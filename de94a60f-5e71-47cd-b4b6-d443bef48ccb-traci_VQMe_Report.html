Seven more years feels like forever when you're already running on empty

A VQMe Internal Dissonance Report for Traci

Traci,

I've been thinking about what you shared — how you're weighing seven more years against a golden parachute while your health takes the hit. That's not a small decision, and the pull between financial security and personal cost is real.

What struck me in your report is how your thinking patterns might be making this choice feel more fixed than it actually is.

When Security Becomes a Prison

You're caught between what makes sense on paper and what your body is telling you. The golden parachute represents real value — I'm not minimizing that. But the way you're framing this decision might be creating more pressure than necessary.

The Pattern

Your strongest bias is Seeing Value in All You Do (10-). This shows up as viewing work purely as "a means to an end" — something you endure to reach a goal. You also have very low clarity in Enjoying Whatever You Are Doing (17-), which aligns with disliking work for years.

Here's what's interesting: you have tremendous clarity in Allocating Your Time & Energy and Valuing Your Self. You know how to make good decisions about your resources, and you recognize your own worth. But those assets aren't getting much airtime when you think about this career situation.

The pattern seems to be: Because the parachute is valuable, I should endure whatever it takes to get there. Seven years is the price.

A Real-Time Example

You're lying awake Sunday night, already dreading Monday morning. The thought of seven more years of this hits you.

  • Trigger: Thinking about seven more years in work you dislike
  • Thought: "I have to stick this out. The parachute is worth it. I just need to survive."
  • Emotion: Trapped, exhausted, resigned
  • Action: Tell yourself to lay low, maybe transfer departments, just get through it
  • Result: Health continues to suffer, and the "means to an end" thinking gets stronger

Thought → Emotion → Action → Result

The trigger — contemplating those seven years — is real. But your response begins with the thought that you have to endure this. That thought, while understandable, may be more limiting than accurate. When you believe survival is your only option, you stop seeing alternatives. The pattern reinforces itself: the worse you feel, the more you believe you're stuck.

What Changes When You Shift Your Thinking

You start asking different questions. Instead of "How do I survive seven more years?" you might ask, "How do I make the best decision for my overall well-being, including financial security?"

You become someone who weighs the real value of the parachute against the real cost to your health — not just financially, but holistically. You stop measuring your situation purely as something to endure and start seeing it as something to actively manage.

Your days feel less like survival mode. You might discover options you hadn't considered: negotiating different terms, finding ways to make the current role more tolerable, or realizing that your health and seven years of your life have a dollar value too.

You stop treating this as a binary choice between financial responsibility and personal well-being. You recognize that protecting your health is financial responsibility.

This isn't about abandoning practical thinking. It's about thinking practically and personally. Your Allocating Your Time & Energy asset can help you weigh the true costs and benefits. Your Valuing Your Self asset reminds you that your health and daily quality of life matter in this equation.
