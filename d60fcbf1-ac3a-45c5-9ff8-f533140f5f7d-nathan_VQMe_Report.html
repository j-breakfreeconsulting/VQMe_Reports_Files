Nathan, what if "not good enough" isn't the real problem?

A VQMe Internal Dissonance Report for Nathan

Nathan,

I read through your VQ measurements and the phrase that stayed with me: "I feel I am not good enough."

That feeling makes sense when you're juggling overwhelm, priorities, and work-life boundaries. When there's too much to manage and not enough time to do it well, it's easy to land on the conclusion that you're falling short.

But what if the issue isn't your capacity? What if it's how you're processing the input coming at you?

When Everything Feels Like Too Much

The Pattern

Your mind has developed a particular sequence when it comes to managing professional demands. You resist outside direction (Openness to Advice and Opinions (10-)) while simultaneously questioning whether what you're doing matters (Seeking Fulfillment (10-)).

This creates an impossible bind. You push away the very input that might help you prioritize or delegate more effectively, yet you're left carrying everything alone — which reinforces the overwhelm that makes you feel "not good enough."

Meanwhile, you're not finding much joy in the day-to-day work (Enjoying Whatever You Are Doing (17-)), even though you have clear values about what matters (Having Courage & Conviction) and strong instincts about how to treat people fairly (Balancing Compassion & Accountability).

The gap between your values and your daily experience is widening. And instead of questioning the system, you're questioning yourself.

A Real-Time Example

Think about the last time someone offered suggestions about how you could manage your workload better — maybe advice about delegation, time-blocking, or saying no to certain commitments.

The trigger: Someone suggests a way to handle your overwhelm.

Your thought: "They don't understand my situation. Their advice doesn't apply to what I'm dealing with."

Emotion: Frustration, maybe a flash of resentment.

Action: You dismiss the input and continue handling things the same way.

Result: The overwhelm persists, and you're left thinking, "I must not be good enough at this."

Thought → Emotion → Action → Result

The trigger — someone's suggestion — is outside your control. But your interpretation of that trigger is where your power lies. When you automatically frame outside input as irrelevant or controlling, you cut yourself off from resources that could actually reduce the overwhelm. The thought that their advice "doesn't apply" keeps you isolated with problems that don't have to be solved alone. And when those problems persist, it's easy to conclude the issue is your capability rather than your approach to receiving support.

What Changes When You Shift Your Thinking

You stop measuring your worth by how much you can handle alone. Instead, you become someone who gets curious about input — not to follow it blindly, but to extract what's useful while staying true to your own judgment.

Your days begin to feel more intentional. You're still dealing with competing priorities, but you're making conscious choices about what deserves your attention rather than reacting to everything that comes at you.

You start seeing your current work differently — not as proof that you're falling short, but as a foundation you're building on. You become someone who can hold both the reality that some things need to change and the truth that you're capable of changing them.

The feeling of "not good enough" gets replaced by something steadier: the recognition that you have strong instincts about what matters and the capability to align your daily choices with those instincts.

Where This Leaves You

At this point, you've seen how resistance to input can actually create more of the overwhelm that feeds self-doubt. That awareness gives you different options than you had before.
