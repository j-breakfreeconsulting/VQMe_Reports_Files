Thought → Emotion → Action → Result

The trigger is external — their response to your program priorities. What you control begins with your thought about what that response means. When you think "they don't understand and I don't have authority," everything above that — the frustration, the over-explaining or pulling back — follows predictably. Trying to change how you feel about their resistance or what you do in meetings without changing the underlying thought about your authority won't create the shift you're looking for.

What Changes When You Shift Your Thinking

When you stop measuring your authority by everyone else's immediate buy-in, something shifts. You become someone who can hold both your expertise and their learning curve without making either one mean something's wrong.

You start distinguishing between your responsibility to communicate clearly and their responsibility to engage. Your days begin to feel less like you're proving the program's worth and more like you're building something valuable, one conversation at a time.

You stop oscillating between "I don't know what I'm doing" and "why don't they get it?" Instead, you recognize that introducing something entirely new requires both patience with yourself as you refine the approach and persistence with others as they integrate new priorities.

The program becomes successful not because everyone immediately embraces it, but because you're consistently clear about its value while allowing others the time they need to see it too.
