Jonathan, what if overwhelm isn't a time problem?

A VQMe Internal Dissonance Report for Jonathan

Jonathan,

I've been looking through your VQ Profile, and something stood out immediately. You identified time challenges — overwhelm, priorities, delegation, work-life boundaries — as what you're working through. That makes sense given what most of us experience in demanding professional environments.

But here's what I noticed in your cognitive pattern: the pressure you're feeling might not be coming from having too much to do. It might be coming from how you're thinking about what you have to do.

When Everything Feels Urgent but Nothing Feels Fulfilling

You're carrying ten cognitive biases — Openness to Advice and Opinions (10-), Seeking Fulfillment (10-), Enjoying Whatever You Are Doing (17-), Seeing Value in All You Do (24-) — each one creating its own form of mental friction around work and worth.

The pattern that emerges is someone who's simultaneously resistant to external input while also feeling disconnected from the intrinsic value of their daily work. You're working hard but not necessarily working from a place that feels aligned or energizing.

The Pattern

You're running two competing scripts. One says "don't let others control or impose on me" (Openness to Advice and Opinions (10-)), while the other whispers "life isn't always the way I would like it to be" (Seeking Fulfillment (10-)).

This creates a particular kind of professional tension: you want autonomy in how you work, but you also feel trapped by the work you're doing. You resist being directed, but you also feel like you're "playing the hand you've been dealt" rather than actively choosing your path (Enjoying Whatever You Are Doing (17-)).

The result is that you end up seeing much of what you do as "a means to an end" (Seeing Value in All You Do (24-)) rather than something inherently meaningful. Work becomes something to get through rather than something that energizes you.

A Real-Time Example

Think about the last time you looked at your calendar and felt that familiar surge of overwhelm.

  • Trigger: Seeing your packed schedule for the week
  • Thought: "I have to do all of this, but none of it feels like what I really want to be doing"
  • Emotion: Frustration mixed with resignation
  • Action: You push through the tasks mechanically, probably more efficiently than you give yourself credit for, but without much engagement
  • Result: You complete what needs to be done, but you feel drained rather than accomplished, reinforcing the sense that work is something you endure rather than enjoy

Thought → Emotion → Action → Result

The trigger — your packed schedule — is external and largely outside your control. But your interpretation of that trigger is where your leverage begins. When you think "I have to do this but don't want to," you're already setting up resistance that makes everything feel harder. The thought creates the emotion of frustrated resignation, which leads to mechanical action, which produces the result of feeling drained rather than energized. This reinforces the original thought pattern.

What Changes When You Shift Your Thinking

You stop measuring your day by how much you got through and start measuring it by how present you were while getting it through. You become someone who finds ways to engage genuinely with work, even when the work itself isn't your first choice.

Your Having Courage & Conviction and Balancing Compassion & Accountability become the foundation for making conscious choices about how you show up, rather than simply reacting to what's in front of you.

You start asking different questions: "What choice can I make in this moment to create the greatest net value?" rather than "How do I get through everything on my list?" The overwhelm doesn't disappear, but it stops feeling like evidence that your life is off track.

Your days begin to feel less like something happening to you and more like something you're actively creating, even within existing constraints.

Where This Leaves You

At this point, you've seen how certain thinking patterns tend to create the emotions and actions that lead to feeling overwhelmed and disconnected from your work. That awareness gives you more options than you had before.
